## Task 1: A-Priori and Association Rules
By the anti-monotone property of support, a pair $\{i, j\}$ cannot appear in more baskets than either of its component singletons $i$ or $j$. By pruning all infrequent items in Pass 1, A-Priori avoids initializing the full $\binom{n}{2} = 1,999,000$ pair counters for $n=2,000$ items. In association rule mining, high confidence alone is insufficient: if a consequent $j$ appears in almost every basket (e.g., milk), a rule $i \to j$ will exhibit high confidence regardless of correlation. Lift measures the ratio of observed confidence to the consequent's baseline frequency; only rules with lift significantly greater than 1 represent genuine positive associations rather than ubiquitous products.

## Task 2: Candidate Explosion
Halving the support threshold from 400 to 200 multiplies candidate pair counters by 5.54x ($10,585 \to 58,626$), and halving from 200 to 100 multiplies them by 4.67x ($58,626 \to 273,701$). This growth is far more aggressive than doubling because candidate pair space scales quadratically as $\binom{|L_1|}{2} \approx \frac{1}{2} |L_1|^2$ with the size of surviving singletons. At support $\le 25$, the counters saturate at 893,456, exhausting over 102 MB of Python dictionary memory. In stark contrast, true frequent pairs grow smoothly (249 at support 400 to 6,397 at support 50). At support 50, 99.22% of all counters allocated in Pass 2 were squandered on pairs that never met the support threshold, making naive threshold lowering prohibitive.

## Task 3: PCY Memory Accounting and Hash Filtering
During Pass 1, counting individual singletons consumes negligible memory ($2,000$ integers), leaving the vast majority of RAM unused. The PCY algorithm exploits this spare memory by hashing all co-occurring pairs into $1,000,003$ hash buckets during Pass 1. Since any pair landing in a bucket with count $< s$ cannot possibly be frequent, we collapse the integer bucket array into a compact 1-byte/bit flag array (`bytearray` taking only $\approx 1$ MB) before Pass 2 begins and discard the original counters. In Pass 2, candidate counters are only allocated for pairs whose hash bucket was marked frequent. This pruned peak counters from 820,259 down to 10,631—an outstanding 98.7% reduction—while identifying the exact same 6,397 frequent pairs and executing in 1.89 seconds (faster than the baseline's 2.63 seconds).
