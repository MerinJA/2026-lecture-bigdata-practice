# Observations — Week 3 · Finding Similar Items (LSH)

### Task 1 · Implementation: MinHash and LSH
- **Single pass over rows:** In Big Data applications, the characteristic binary matrix does not fit in main memory and is streamed row by row from disk or network. Scanning column by column would require re-reading the entire massive dataset $C$ separate times ($O(C)$ full disk passes). Walking the rows once updates the minhash signature of every active column ($r \in \text{col}$) on the fly in a single sequential streaming pass ($O(R)$ I/O).
- **R5 decision on leftover rows:** When the signature length is not evenly divisible by the number of bands (`len(sig) % bands != 0`), an explicit `ValueError` is raised. Silently truncating or dropping leftover hash components would discard computed entropy without warning the user.
- **S1–S4 estimate gap (1.0 vs true 2/3):** With only 2 hash functions, the MinHash collision probability estimator has a huge standard error ($O(1/\sqrt{k})$) and can only produce estimates of 0.0, 0.5, or 1.0. To narrow the estimate towards the true 2/3, we must increase the number of hash functions $k$ (e.g., $k=128$). The cost scales linearly in $O(k)$ for both hashing computation time and signature storage.

### Task 2 · Measurement: Crossover on Hardware
- **Machine & crossover point:** Benchmarked on Windows 11 with an Intel Core Ultra 7 (Intel64 Family 6 Model 170) and 16 GB RAM using Python 3.12.6. The crossover point occurs between $n = 500$ and $n = 1,000$ documents. Below $n = 500$, brute force is faster ($0.71\text{ s}$ vs $1.18\text{ s}$ for LSH) because the fixed overhead of computing 128 hashes and indexing 32 bands exceeds the cost of a few pairwise intersections. At $n = 1,000$, LSH takes the lead ($2.40\text{ s}$ vs $2.86\text{ s}$).
- **Quadratic scaling check (A4):** Doubling $n$ multiplies brute-force runtime by approximately 4: $n=250 \to 500$ ($\times 3.94$), $n=500 \to 1,000$ ($\times 4.03$), $n=1,000 \to 2,000$ ($\times 4.11$). The $O(n^2)$ complexity is strictly confirmed.
- **Unpleasant size & bottleneck:** At $n = 4,000$, brute force becomes genuinely painful, taking $65.24\text{ s}$ (over a minute) for 8 million comparisons, while LSH finishes in $14.17\text{ s}$. Execution time ran out first: memory consumption for brute force remained minimal ($7.3\text{ KiB}$) while wall-clock time exploded quadratically.

### Task 3 · Improvement: Scaling and S-Curve
- **Parameters $n$, $b$, and S-curve step:** We chose $n = 128$ hashes and $b = 32$ bands ($r = 4$ rows per band). The S-curve inflection point (step) sits at $(1/b)^{1/r} = (1/32)^{1/4} \approx 0.420$. This step is intentionally placed below the target similarity threshold $s = 0.60$.
- **Detection probability & recall:** At similarity $s = 0.60$, the candidate collision probability is $1 - (1 - s^r)^b = 1 - (1 - 0.6^4)^{32} \approx 0.988$ ($98.8\%$). This guarantees $100.0\%$ recall on the planted benchmark while eliminating $99.99\%$ of comparisons (only 169 similarity calls instead of 2,246,140), achieving the `strong` grade.
- **Moving the step the wrong way:** Shifting the step above the threshold (e.g., $b = 16, r = 8 \implies \text{step} \approx (1/16)^{1/8} \approx 0.707 > 0.6$) causes the collision probability at $s=0.60$ to collapse to $1 - (1 - 0.6^8)^{16} \approx 0.026$ ($2.6\%$), destroying recall (less than 5% of true pairs retrieved).
- **Free hashing simplification:** The harness does not bill for hashing. This simplification stops being realistic at extreme scale (hundreds of millions of documents with large vocabulary), or in distributed architectures where computing, storing, and shuffling high-dimensional minhash signatures over the network becomes the dominant operational bottleneck.
